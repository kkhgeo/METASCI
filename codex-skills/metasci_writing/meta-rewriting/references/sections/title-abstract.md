# Titles, Abstracts, and Keywords

Use with [the shared principles](../principles.md), including §§6.3–6.4 on available context, preservation, and disclosure. Apply section-level completeness checks only when the relevant section is supplied.

**Basis notes:** Original IDs point to the archived guide linked below. They identify practical guidance and provenance, not newly verified empirical findings. “Editorial” identifies a new diagnostic or qualification in this revision. Diagnostic questions are aids to judgment, not a mandatory sequence.

## Purpose

Identify the study accurately and summarize its contribution for the intended audience.

## 1. Title

### TA1. Identify the subject and scope

Name the central subject and the relationship, method, or contribution that distinguishes the study. Include the setting, period, population, or design when it defines the scope. Prefer precise scientific terms to promotional wording.

**Scope check:** What study would a reader expect from the title alone? Compare that expectation with the actual design and evidence.

**Basis:** Original section-structure-27, -48, -49; remined-title-3, -5, -7. Scope check: editorial.

### TA2. Choose a defensible title form

A descriptive title identifies the topic; a finding-based title states a result. Choose according to the study and publication conventions. Assess whether a finding-based title implies unsupported certainty, causality, or generality.

**Basis:** Original remined-title-1 and -8, reconciled editorially as alternatives rather than a universal preference.

## 2. Abstract

### TA3. Build a connected summary

A useful progression is context and objective → essential approach → principal findings → conclusion. Adapt the form to the article type and journal.

Explain the specific problem, the approach needed to interpret the findings, and the findings that answer the selected objective.

**Alignment check:** For each objective included in the abstract, locate a corresponding result. For each concluding claim, locate the supporting result. Missing links may require changing what the abstract selects rather than adding more sentences.

**Basis:** Original section-structure-6, -52, -53, -54; remined-abstract-10. Alignment check: editorial.

### TA4. Select representative findings

Prioritize findings that answer the main question, not merely the largest differences or smallest p-values. Include important null or contrary findings when their omission would change the overall conclusion.

Use quantitative evidence suited to the analysis. Retain the comparison, conditions, sample basis, or uncertainty needed to interpret a selected number.

**Basis:** Original section-structure-25 and -53; editorial extension on representative selection.

### TA5. Check independence and fidelity

The abstract should be understandable on its own and agree with the manuscript. Define indispensable unfamiliar terms and avoid references that require unavailable context.

**Fidelity check:** Can each reported method, result, and implication be located in the manuscript? When only the abstract is available, assess its internal coherence and identify the unverified manuscript comparison.

**Basis:** Original section-structure-21; remined-abstract-1, -9, -15. Check: editorial.

## 3. Keywords and format

### TA6. Match discovery terms and publication requirements

Choose accurate topic terms that a relevant reader would search for. Use controlled vocabularies where appropriate; assess title-word overlap by usefulness rather than prohibition.

When formatting is requested, consult current instructions for word limits, headings, keyword counts, running titles, citations, and design-specific reporting. Venue-specific templates belong with those instructions.

**Basis:** Original section-structure-31; remined-abstract-10, -12, -13 and remined-title-10; editorial qualification of fixed counts and keyword exclusions.
