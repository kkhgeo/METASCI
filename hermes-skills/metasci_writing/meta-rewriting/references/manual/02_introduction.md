### `section-structure-1` — convergence 13
Build the introduction as a funnel — establish the territory, establish the niche (gap), then occupy it with your contribution.

> "the so-called CaRS model (Create-A-Research-Space) that are: establish a research \"territory\", establish a research \"niche\" and occupy the niche (Swales and Feak, 1994)."

### `section-structure-5` — convergence 10
Make one specific research question the paper's central organizing principle and spell it out explicitly.

> "A good research paper addresses a specific research question. The research question—or study objective or main research hypothesis—is the central organizing principle of the paper. Whatever relates to the research question belongs in the paper; the rest doesn't."

**Sources (10):** perneger-2004, fisher-2013, tweets-03, hon-uf, b3-lund, b5-schulzrinne, tweets-04, koons-2019, b1-iufro, hengl-2002
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review

### `section-structure-7` — convergence 10
Establish novelty explicitly — state what the work adds to or refutes in existing knowledge, and revisit in the Discussion whether the gap claimed in the Introduction was actually filled.

> "A study does not necessarily have to break completely new ground, but it should extend previous knowledge in a useful way, or alternatively refute existing knowledge."

**Sources (10):** perneger-2004, hengl-2002, b5-schulzrinne, b7-nature, hon-uf, b1-iufro, koons-2019, zhu-2026, tweets-01a, ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review · also filed under: 05_discussion.md

### `section-structure-9` — convergence 10
Answer "So what?" — state why the problem matters, what the results mean when all analyses are taken together, and how they apply in practice.

> "Introduction (brief!): introduce problem, outline solution; the statement of the problem should include a clear statement why the problem is important (or interesting)."

**Sources (10):** perneger-2004, b5-schulzrinne, hon-uf, b1-iufro, b6-fess, tweets-04, fisher-2013, b7-nature, ecarnot-2015, saver-2007
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review · also filed under: 05_discussion.md

### `section-structure-18` — convergence 7
A literature review synthesizes and critiques by idea, not by article, determining what is known and unknown to expose the gap.

> "This chapter, however, should not merely string together what other researchers have found. Rather, you should discuss and analyze the body of knowledge with the ultimate goal of determining what is known and is not known about the topic."

**Sources (7):** hon-uf, tweets-01a, tweets-01c, pautasso-2013, koons-2019, b1-iufro, zhu-2026
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review

### `section-structure-23` — convergence 7
State objectives explicitly in the introduction — specific, measurable, naming the exact parameter and how it will be assessed, matching the protocol, never tautological or confused with methods.

> "Objectives must be tangible, specific, concrete, measurable and achievable in the time period of the project."

**Sources (7):** perneger-2004, b1-iufro, hon-uf, tweets-04, hengl-2002, ecarnot-2015, saver-2007

### `section-structure-32` — convergence 5
Touch prior work briefly in the introduction to show its insufficiency; defer the full review to a Related Work or literature section.

> "While it is neither necessary nor desirable to provide a full-blown review of the literature as a prelude to the study, it is helpful to situate the study within some larger field of enquiry."

> "One or two sentences providing a basic introduction to the field, comprehensible to a scientist in any discipline."

### `section-structure-40` — convergence 4
State hypotheses explicitly, including rivals, and report results and discussion in the same order as the hypotheses and methods were given.

> "give in same order as hypotheses."

### `section-structure-55` — convergence 1
The introduction's first job is to hook the reader — above all the reviewer — turning the gap statement into a measured sales pitch for the study's added value.

> "The introduction is of prime importance in grabbing the reader's attention. In particular during the review process, the introduction must get the reviewer \"hooked\", wanting to read more, and thinking to themselves, \"How come I never thought of this?\"."

**Sources (1):** ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review

### `section-structure-56` — convergence 1
After the gap, close the introduction in a fixed order: the working hypothesis, then the objectives, then very briefly the strategy implemented to achieve them.

> "Having explained how your study is going to contribute something new and useful, you should clearly state your working hypothesis, followed by your objective(s), and very briefly, the strategy implemented to achieve these goals"

**Sources (1):** ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review

### `remined-intro-1` — convergence 1
A research question must be debatable. A question whose answer is simply right or wrong is a factual query, not a research question — the gap you name has to be one that argument can occupy.

> "questions that will lead to answers that are either right or wrong and therefore not debatable are (usually) not suitable as research questions."

**Sources (1):** b3-lund
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review

### `remined-intro-2` — convergence 1
Test the research question against three attributes — specificity, originality, and relevance to a broad scientific community — and where the design allows, phrase it as a possible association between X and Y in population Z.

> "What is a good research question? The key attributes are: (i) specificity; (ii) originality or novelty; and (iii) general relevance to a broad scientific community."

**Sources (1):** perneger-2004
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `perneger-2004-5`, `perneger-2004-6`. Second quote: "The research question should be precise and not merely identify a general area of inquiry. It can often (but not always) be expressed in terms of a possible association between X and Y in a population Z."

### `remined-intro-7` — convergence 1
Anti-pattern — the development chronicle. An introduction that recounts what your lab built, which earlier versions you struggled with, and what the system is called is a history of your group, not of the problem. Open on the problem and the contribution.

> "Example bad introduction: 'Here at the institute for computer research, me and my colleagues have created the SUPERGP system and have applied it to several toy problems. We had previously fumbled with earlier versions of SUPERGPSYSTEM for a while.'"

**Sources (1):** b5-schulzrinne
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b5-schulzrinne-83`; quoted in the source as an example of what not to write.

### `remined-intro-11` — convergence 1

### `remined-intro-14` — convergence 1

### `remined-intro-19` — convergence 1
The review has to establish that the work is new, significant, and not a repetition of something already done under similar conditions. An incomplete review that fails this is a named cause of rejection — your position and publication record will not stand in for it.

> "The literature review was incomplete and failed to establish that the proposed research was new and significant and not repetitive, i.e., the work being proposed had already been carried out in similar ecological and socio-economic conditions."

### `remined-intro-22` — convergence 1
Write objectives with verbs that name an expected outcome — *to increase*, *to decrease*, *to reduce* — rather than vague ones like *to provide*, *to establish*, *to create*. And set them off visually with bullets, numbers, or indentation so they are not buried in prose.

> "The use of such terms as \"to increase\", \"to decrease\", \"to reduce\" are more convincing and indicative of anticipated outcomes than such terms as \"to provide\", \"to establish\", or \"to create\"."

**Sources (1):** b1-iufro
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b1-iufro-46`, `b1-iufro-23`.

### `remined-intro-23` — convergence 1

> "Avoid the trap of setting hypotheses or objectives in sequence such that one objective is dependent on a particular outcome of another objective. This can be very dangerous indeed, and reviewers usually look for this."

### `remined-intro-24` — convergence 1
Keep objectives distinct from goals, and hypotheses testable. Goals are conceptual, ultimate, abstract; objectives are tangible and time-bounded. A proposed answer that cannot be tested is useless for further investigation.

> "Do not confuse objectives with goals. The latter are conceptual, ultimate and more abstract."

### `remined-intro-26` — convergence 2
The introduction has five jobs: define the context, identify the area of study, define the topic and key terms, state the purpose, and signpost what is covered where.
