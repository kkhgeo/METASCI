### `section-structure-6` — convergence 10
Compose the abstract in a fixed sequence: background/objective, methods, results with concrete numbers, conclusion and implications.

> "This study examines [topic/problem]. We used [method] to investigate [specific question]. Results showed that [main finding with specific data]. These findings suggest [implication/conclusion]. This has important applications for [practical use or field]."

**Sources (10):** fisher-2013, tweets-01a, tweets-02, tweets-04, tullu-2019, b7-nature, hengl-2002, b1-iufro, b3-lund, ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords

## Title

### `remined-title-1` — convergence 1
Prefer a **descriptive (neutral)** title for a research article. It carries the essential elements — subjects, design, intervention, comparison, outcome — without revealing the main result, so the reader interprets the findings with an open mind. A **declarative** title states the finding and both spends the reader's curiosity and signals author bias. An **interrogative** title sensationalises: more downloads, fewer citations, and distracting for a research article (acceptable at times for a review).

> "This title states the main finding of the study in the title itself; it reduces the curiosity of the reader, may point toward a bias on the part of the author, and hence is best avoided. (…) Though a query in the title has the ability to sensationalize the topic, and has more downloads (but less citations), it can be distracting to the reader and is again best avoided for a research article (but can, at times, be used for a review article)."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-11`, `tullu-2019-53`, `tullu-2019-54`.

### `remined-title-2` — convergence 1
Draft the title in steps: describe the paper in about three sentences containing the key scientific terms but no results; join them into one sentence; strip redundant words, adjectives, and phrases; then edit for accuracy and concision.

> "The author should describe the paper in about three sentences, avoiding the results and ensuring that these sentences contain important scientific words/keywords that describe the main contents and subject of the paper. Then the author should join the sentences to form a single sentence, shorten the length (by removing redundant words or adjectives or phrases), and finally edit the title (thus drafted) to make it more accurate, concise (about 10–15 words), and precise."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-12`. On the word count see `DECISIONS.md` §D4 — sources disagree.

### `remined-title-3` — convergence 3
Cut the empty opening formula. "Study of…", "An investigation of…", "The analysis of…", "Effect of…", "Influence of…", "New method…", "novel", "performance evaluation", "architecture" — these appear in almost every paper, carry no information, and are not what anyone searches for. If there are effects, say what they are.

> "Avoid common phrases like 'novel', 'performance evaluation' and 'architecture', since almost every paper does a performance evaluation of some architecture and it better be novel."

**Sources (3):** b5-schulzrinne, hengl-2002, tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b5-schulzrinne-19`, `hengl-2002-23`, `tullu-2019-60`. Also `ecarnot-2015-57`: "Avoid terms such as ''a report of...'' or ''the effects of...''. If there are effects, state what they are!" Exception (`b5-schulzrinne-79`): if performance evaluation genuinely is the core, still prefer something specific — "Delay measurements of X".

### `remined-title-5` — convergence 2
Keep abbreviations out of the title unless they are universally read (HIV, DNA, ECG). A reader who does not recognise the abbreviation skips the article, and nonstandard forms break indexing. > "Abbreviations (except the standard or commonly interpreted ones such as HIV, AIDS, DNA, RNA, CDC, FDA, ECG, and EEG) or acronyms should be avoided in the title, as a reader not familiar with them may skip such an article and nonstandard abbreviations may create problems in indexing the article."

## Abstract

### `remined-abstract-1` — convergence 4
The abstract must stand alone. It circulates by itself in bibliographic databases, so it has to be comprehensible without the full text — and neither may the body depend on anything stated only in the abstract.

> "The abstract should be considered as an independent document, so that the abstract does not rely upon any material in the body of the report and, similarly, the body of the report does not rely upon any material in the abstract."

## The Nature summary-paragraph template

### `remined-abstract-18` — convergence 1
A seven-part template for a single-paragraph summary, each part with its own required reading level:

1. **1–2 sentences** — basic introduction to the field, *comprehensible to a scientist in any discipline*
2. **2–3 sentences** — more detailed background, *comprehensible to scientists in related disciplines*
3. **1 sentence** — the general problem this study addresses
4. **1 sentence** — the main result, using "here we show" or its equivalent
5. **2–3 sentences** — what that result reveals *in direct comparison to what was previously thought*, or what it adds
6. **1–2 sentences** — the results placed in a more general context
