## Title

### `remined-title-1` — convergence 1
Prefer a **descriptive (neutral)** title for a research article. It carries the essential elements — subjects, design, intervention, comparison, outcome — without revealing the main result, so the reader interprets the findings with an open mind. A **declarative** title states the finding and both spends the reader's curiosity and signals author bias. An **interrogative** title sensationalises: more downloads, fewer citations, and distracting for a research article (acceptable at times for a review).

> "This title states the main finding of the study in the title itself; it reduces the curiosity of the reader, may point toward a bias on the part of the author, and hence is best avoided. (…) Though a query in the title has the ability to sensationalize the topic, and has more downloads (but less citations), it can be distracting to the reader and is again best avoided for a research article (but can, at times, be used for a review article)."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-11`, `tullu-2019-53`, `tullu-2019-54`.

### `remined-title-2` — convergence 1
Draft the title in steps: describe the paper in about three sentences containing the key scientific terms but no results; join them into one sentence; strip redundant words, adjectives, and phrases; then edit for accuracy and concision.

> "The author should describe the paper in about three sentences, avoiding the results and ensuring that these sentences contain important scientific words/keywords that describe the main contents and subject of the paper. Then the author should join the sentences to form a single sentence, shorten the length (by removing redundant words or adjectives or phrases), and finally edit the title (thus drafted) to make it more accurate, concise (about 10–15 words), and precise."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-12`. On the word count see `DECISIONS.md` §D4 — sources disagree.

### `remined-title-3` — convergence 3
Cut the empty opening formula. "Study of…", "An investigation of…", "The analysis of…", "Effect of…", "Influence of…", "New method…", "novel", "performance evaluation", "architecture" — these appear in almost every paper, carry no information, and are not what anyone searches for. If there are effects, say what they are.

> "Avoid common phrases like 'novel', 'performance evaluation' and 'architecture', since almost every paper does a performance evaluation of some architecture and it better be novel."

**Sources (3):** b5-schulzrinne, hengl-2002, tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b5-schulzrinne-19`, `hengl-2002-23`, `tullu-2019-60`. Also `ecarnot-2015-57`: "Avoid terms such as ''a report of...'' or ''the effects of...''. If there are effects, state what they are!" Exception (`b5-schulzrinne-79`): if performance evaluation genuinely is the core, still prefer something specific — "Delay measurements of X".

### `remined-title-4` — convergence 1
Use adjectives that name what is distinctive about the work — reliable, scalable, high-performance, robust, low-complexity, low-cost.

> "Use adjectives that describe the distinctive features of your work, e.g., reliable, scalable, high-performance, robust, low-complexity, or low-cost."

**Sources (1):** b5-schulzrinne
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b5-schulzrinne-20`.

### `remined-title-5` — convergence 2
Keep abbreviations out of the title unless they are universally read (HIV, DNA, ECG). A reader who does not recognise the abbreviation skips the article, and nonstandard forms break indexing. Drop an abbreviation whose full form already appears in the title. Heavy jargon and chemical formulae have the same effect.

> "Abbreviations (except the standard or commonly interpreted ones such as HIV, AIDS, DNA, RNA, CDC, FDA, ECG, and EEG) or acronyms should be avoided in the title, as a reader not familiar with them may skip such an article and nonstandard abbreviations may create problems in indexing the article."

**Sources (2):** tullu-2019, b5-schulzrinne
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-15`, `tullu-2019-16`, `tullu-2019-61`, `b5-schulzrinne-18`.

### `remined-title-6` — convergence 1
Avoid whimsical, amusing, or clever titles. They are missed or misread by busy readers, taken less seriously, and cited less often.

> "Whimsical, amusing, or clever titles, though initially appealing, may be missed or misread by the busy reader and very short titles may miss the essential scientific words ('keywords') used by the indexing agencies to catch and categorize the paper. Also, amusing or hilarious titles may be taken less seriously by the readers and may be cited less often."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords

### `remined-title-7` — convergence 1
Include the study period, sample size, or place of study only where it adds scientific value — for place, only when population characteristics, socioeconomic conditions, or cultural practice plausibly bear on the outcome. Where the journal requires the design in the title, put it after a colon.

> "Place of the study and sample size should be mentioned only if it adds to the scientific value of the title"

### `remined-title-8` — convergence 1
Choose the sentence construct deliberately: **nominal** (theme only), **compound** (a subtitle adding context, design, location, period, or sample size), or **full-sentence** (longer, and signalling a degree of certainty about the result). Use subtitles sparingly.

> "From a sentence construct point of view, titles may be nominal (capturing only the main theme of the study), compound (with subtitles to provide additional relevant information such as context, design, location/country, temporal aspect, sample size, importance, and a provocative or a literary; for example, see the title of this review), or full-sentence titles (which are longer and indicate an added degree of certainty of the results)."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-55`. "Subtitles should be used sparingly" — `ecarnot-2015-57`.

### `remined-title-9` — convergence 1
Run the title against a content checklist. **SPICED** — Setting, Population, Intervention, Condition, End-point, Design. In clinical work **PICO** — Patients, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcome.

> "Title should be SPICED, that is, it should include Setting, Population, Intervention, Condition, End-point, and Design."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-20`, `tullu-2019-13`.

### `remined-title-10` — convergence 1
Supply a running head of 40–50 characters — an abridged title, standard abbreviations permitted, that helps the reader navigate the paper.

> "This is an abridged version of the main title of up to 40–50 characters, may have standard abbreviations, and helps the reader to navigate through the paper."

**Sources (1):** tullu-2019
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-19`.

### `remined-title-11` — convergence 2
Draft the title after the body and the abstract exist, and do not finalise it in a hurry — the constraints genuinely conflict. Get an impartial colleague to read it before you settle.

> "It may be worthwhile to take an opinion from an impartial colleague before finalizing the title. Thus, multiple factors (which are, at times, a bit conflicting or contrasting) need to be considered while formulating a title, and hence this should not be done in a hurry."

**Sources (2):** tullu-2019, ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `tullu-2019-18`, `tullu-2019-10`, `ecarnot-2015-58`. `ecarnot-2015`: study titles in the top journals of your field for models, and check the journal's length limit — "keeping it short is harder than coming up with a 4-line title."

## Abstract

### `remined-abstract-1` — convergence 4
The abstract must stand alone. It circulates by itself in bibliographic databases, so it has to be comprehensible without the full text — and neither may the body depend on anything stated only in the abstract.

> "The abstract should be considered as an independent document, so that the abstract does not rely upon any material in the body of the report and, similarly, the body of the report does not rely upon any material in the abstract."

**Sources (4):** fisher-2013, tullu-2019, ecarnot-2015, hengl-2002
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `fisher-2013-9`, `tullu-2019-22`, `ecarnot-2015-47`, `hengl-2002-25`.

### `remined-abstract-2` — convergence 3
Write the abstract last. You cannot summarise what you have not finished, and drafting it at the end is what keeps it consistent with the body.

> "Though some authors prefer to draft the abstract initially, followed by the main text of the paper, it is recommended to draft the abstract in the end to maintain accuracy and conformity with the main text of the paper"

### `remined-abstract-3` — convergence 1
Do not write "in this paper" in the abstract. What other paper would it be?

> "Avoid use of 'in this paper' in the abstract. What other paper would you be talking about here?"

### `remined-abstract-4` — convergence 2
Cut general motivation from the abstract. You do not have to justify the importance of your field or define its standard concepts — the abstract has no room for it and the reader does not need it.

> "Avoid general motivation in the abstract. You do not have to justify the importance of the Internet or explain what QoS is."

### `remined-abstract-5` — convergence 2
Lead with results, not only with the problem. Many readers decide from the abstract alone whether to open the paper, so the principal findings — with their summary numbers — must be in it.

> "Highlight not just the problem, but also the principal results. Many people read abstracts and then decide whether to bother with the rest of the paper."

### `remined-abstract-6` — convergence 1
The abstract is indexed by search engines, so the terms that identify your work — the protocol or system name, the field label — must appear in it.

> "Since the abstract will be used by search engines, be sure that terms that identify your work are found there."

### `remined-abstract-8` — convergence 1
Citations are barred from the abstract, but naming prior work is not. Referring to it by author, abbreviation, or RFC number is acceptable, if uncommon — "Our algorithm is based upon the work by Smith and Wesson."

> "It is acceptable, although not common, to identify work by author, abbreviation or RFC number. (For example, 'Our algorithm is based upon the work by Smith and Wesson.')"

### `remined-abstract-9` — convergence 1
Do not introduce anything in the abstract that is not in the paper. It summarises existing content only — and when you revise the manuscript, revise the abstract to match.

> "Don't introduce new information that isn't in your paper. The abstract summarizes existing content only."

### `remined-abstract-10` — convergence 1
The structural form of the abstract is the journal's decision, not the author's. Structured (four-point or eight-point) or unstructured, and which subheadings — check the instructions. Meta-analyses, educational research, quality-improvement work, reviews, and case studies may need their own subheadings.

> "Anyway, the choice of the type of the abstract and the subheadings of a structured abstract depend on the particular journal style and is not left to the author's wish."

### `remined-abstract-11` — convergence 1
For original research write an **informative** abstract — a complete summary of the contents that truly reflects the research done. A **descriptive** abstract (75–150 words) only says what the paper contains and forces the reader to the full text; it is rarely appropriate for original research.

> "Descriptive abstracts are short (75–150 words), only portray what the paper contains without providing any more details; the reader has to read the full paper to know about its contents and are rarely used for original research papers."

### `remined-abstract-12` — convergence 1
Clinical trial abstracts must carry the essential CONSORT items, and comparable reporting guidelines exist for observational studies, diagnostic-accuracy studies, and others. Find yours at the EQUATOR network (www.equator-network.org). ICMJE additionally recommends printing the trial registration number at the end of the abstract.

> "Clinical trial abstracts need to include the essential items mentioned in the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards Of Reporting Trials) guidelines. Similar guidelines exist for various other types of studies, including observational studies and for studies of diagnostic accuracy."

### `remined-abstract-13` — convergence 1
Supply three to ten keywords after the abstract, preferably drawn from the field's controlled vocabulary (MeSH in biomedicine), since they drive indexing.

> "Appropriate 'Keywords' (three to ten words or phrases) should follow the abstract and should be preferably chosen from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) list of the U.S. National Library of Medicine (https://meshb.nlm.nih.gov/search) and are used for indexing purposes."

### `remined-abstract-14` — convergence 2
Design the title and abstract for three audiences at once: the **editor**, deciding whether to send it out at all; the **reviewer**, who receives only these two before agreeing to review; and the **reader**, for whom these are often the only freely available parts of the paper. Most readers never reach the full text.

> "Both the title and the abstract are the most important parts of a research paper – for editors (to decide whether to process the paper for further review), for reviewers (to get an initial impression of the paper), and for the readers (as these may be the only parts of the paper available freely and hence, read widely)."

> "Can someone understand my research from this abstract alone? Does it accurately represent my paper? Would I want to read the full paper based on this summary? Is every word necessary?"

## The Nature summary-paragraph template

### `remined-abstract-18` — convergence 1
A seven-part template for a single-paragraph summary, each part with its own required reading level:

1. **1–2 sentences** — basic introduction to the field, *comprehensible to a scientist in any discipline*
2. **2–3 sentences** — more detailed background, *comprehensible to scientists in related disciplines*
3. **1 sentence** — the general problem this study addresses
4. **1 sentence** — the main result, using "here we show" or its equivalent
5. **2–3 sentences** — what that result reveals *in direct comparison to what was previously thought*, or what it adds
6. **1–2 sentences** — the results placed in a more general context
7. **2–3 sentences** *(optional)* — a broader perspective, again readable by a scientist in any discipline

> "One sentence summarizing the main result (with the words \"here we show\" or their equivalent)."

**Sources (1):** b7-nature
**Scope:** Title, Abstract, Keywords
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b7-nature-1` through `b7-nature-9`. **Length:** the stated cap is up to 300 words when part 7 is included; the source's worked example runs 190 words without it and 250 with — those are the example's actual lengths, not a prescribed range. Note the accessibility arc: broad → narrow → broad again, specified per part rather than for the paragraph as a whole.
