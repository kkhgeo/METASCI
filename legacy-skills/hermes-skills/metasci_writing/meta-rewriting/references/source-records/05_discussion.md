### `section-structure-3` — convergence 11
State limitations, biases, and validity threats honestly and never overstate implications; enumerating your own limits shows awareness and creates room to defend them.

> "The Discussion overstates the implications of the results and does not acknowledge the limitations of the study"

**Sources (11):** perneger-2004, hengl-2002, b3-lund, fisher-2013, hon-uf, tweets-04, b5-schulzrinne, zhu-2026, b1-iufro, tweets-01a, ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Discussion

### `section-structure-7` — convergence 10
Establish novelty explicitly — state what the work adds to or refutes in existing knowledge, and revisit in the Discussion whether the gap claimed in the Introduction was actually filled.

> "A study does not necessarily have to break completely new ground, but it should extend previous knowledge in a useful way, or alternatively refute existing knowledge."

**Sources (10):** perneger-2004, hengl-2002, b5-schulzrinne, b7-nature, hon-uf, b1-iufro, koons-2019, zhu-2026, tweets-01a, ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Discussion · also filed under: 02_introduction.md

### `section-structure-9` — convergence 10
Answer "So what?" — state why the problem matters, what the results mean when all analyses are taken together, and how they apply in practice.

> "Introduction (brief!): introduce problem, outline solution; the statement of the problem should include a clear statement why the problem is important (or interesting)."

**Sources (10):** perneger-2004, b5-schulzrinne, hon-uf, b1-iufro, b6-fess, tweets-04, fisher-2013, b7-nature, ecarnot-2015, saver-2007
**Scope:** Discussion · also filed under: 02_introduction.md

### `section-structure-12` — convergence 9
Follow a fixed discussion plan: main findings (in the same wording as the primary objective and endpoint), comparison with prior work, implications, strengths and limitations, future perspectives.

> "State the main findings of the study / Discuss the main results with reference to previous research / Discuss policy and practice implications of the results / Analyse the strengths and limitations of the study / Offer perspectives for future work"

**Sources (9):** perneger-2004, hengl-2002, b3-lund, fisher-2013, tweets-01a, tweets-04, hon-uf, tweets-01c, ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Discussion

### `remined-discussion-1` — convergence 2
The Discussion is where papers are weakest. It allows the most freedom, which makes it the hardest section to write and the one authors most often skimp — even though it is the one that answers "So what?". Write it to a plan rather than freehand.

> "The Discussion section allows the most freedom. This is why the Discussion is the most difficult to write, and is often the weakest part of a paper."

**Sources (2):** perneger-2004, hon-uf
**Scope:** Discussion

### `remined-discussion-2` — convergence 1
Do not recapitulate the results — make statements about them. And do not hide results that came out unexpectedly; those are often the most important thing in the paper.

> "do not recapitulate results but make statements; ... - do not hide unexpected results - they can be the most important;"

**Sources (1):** hengl-2002
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `hengl-2002-32`.

### `remined-discussion-3` — convergence 1
Interpretation fails in two directions. Under-interpreting just repeats the results; over-interpreting quietly promotes a sample observation into a population claim — 20 of 25 patients becomes "80% of patients who receive drug X". That shift is subtle and it misrepresents your own data.

> "Pay attention when interpreting not to simply repeat the results, or at the other end of the scale, not to over-interpret... it is not accurate to indicate in the discussion that ''80% of patients who receive drug X have intracranial hemorrhage''. This is a subtle shift in interpretation that belies that original data."

**Sources (1):** ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `ecarnot-2015-38`.

### `remined-discussion-4` — convergence 2
Place your results against other reports, and when they disagree, offer plausible explanations — differences in circumstances, populations, or approach. Contrast is the work; a list of adjacent citations is not.

> "Putting your results in perspective with other reports is an important part of the discussion. How do your results compare to other reports in the literature? If your findings are different, do you have any plausible explanations? What are the possible discrepancies in circumstances, populations or approaches that may explain why you observed what you observed?"

**Sources (2):** ecarnot-2015, fisher-2013
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `ecarnot-2015-39`, `fisher-2013-24`.

### `remined-discussion-5` — convergence 1
Criticize other people's work diplomatically. Rather than naming a weakness in their study, state the corresponding strength of yours — "our study was adequately powered to detect this" instead of "Smith's study was underpowered". The implication is obvious and the author may be your reviewer.

> "Remember, it pays to be diplomatic when criticizing the work of others. Instead of pointing out weaknesses in other people's work, reformulate so as to present the strong points of your own work – the implication will be obvious, without you having to explicitly criticize your peers' publications."

**Sources (1):** ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `ecarnot-2015-41`. Section-specific case of `paragraph-logic-28` in `00_universal.md`.

### `remined-discussion-6` — convergence 1
Do not be afraid to report negative results. A well-designed, well-conducted study contributes to the evidence base whether or not it found what it hoped for, and negative findings advance a field by questioning received wisdom and refuting anecdote.

> "Do not be afraid to write an article reporting negative results. A well-conducted study that does not yield positive results is always a useful contribution to the current body of evidence... As long as your study has been well designed and conducted appropriately, there is no reason to believe that your findings are not valid, even if they are negative."

**Sources (1):** ecarnot-2015
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `ecarnot-2015-44`.

### `remined-discussion-7` — convergence 1
Discuss both theoretical and practical implications — and present interpretations of the findings, never opinion.

> "When discussing implications, deal with both the theoretical and the practical. Present only interpretations of the findings, not opinion."

### `remined-discussion-8` — convergence 1

### `remined-discussion-10` — convergence 1
Whether a result obtained in one local setting has wider relevance is not self-evident. It has to be argued, with reasons, not assumed by placing it in a general discussion.

> "whether the results of a local quality improvement project have wider relevance requires careful consideration and argument."

**Sources (1):** perneger-2004
**Scope:** Discussion

### `remined-discussion-11` — convergence 1
A correlational or cross-sectional design identifies associations, not causes. Name the reverse-causal reading explicitly rather than letting the prose imply direction.

> "Cross-sectional data allow identification of associations but make it difficult to establish causality; a reverse-causal possibility exists whereby students with higher critical thinking and research skills are themselves more inclined toward transformative AI use."

**Sources (1):** zhu-2026
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `zhu-2026-38`.

### `remined-discussion-12` — convergence 1

### `remined-discussion-13` — convergence 1
Describe the limitations of your method in detail. The cases where it fails are what define its range of applicability — they are information, not embarrassment.

> "Limitations of the algorithm should be described in detail. Interesting cases where an algorithm fails are important in clarifying the range of applicability of an algorithm."

**Sources (1):** b5-schulzrinne
**Scope:** Discussion
**[+] Re-mined 2026-08-09** — from `b5-schulzrinne-59`.

### `remined-discussion-14` — convergence 1
Let the Discussion's shape mirror the form the study claimed for itself — look especially at the Introduction's last paragraph. If you set out three objectives, discuss three; if you posed a hypothesis, argue its acceptance or rejection.

> "In particular, look to the last paragraph of the introduction."

**Sources (1):** fisher-2013
**Scope:** Discussion

### `remined-discussion-17` — OPEN DECISION
How strongly to state conclusions is contested in this corpus and is **not** settled here. `hengl-2002` requires assertion — "We concluded…" rather than "It may be concluded…" — while `tweets-01d` requires hedging — "suggests", "may indicate". **See `DECISIONS.md` §D3 and ask the user before applying either.**

> "\"We concluded... \" instead of \"It may be concluded... \"" — `hengl-2002` · "Hedging: Use cautious language (\"suggests,\" \"may indicate\")." — `tweets-01d`

**Sources (2):** hengl-2002, tweets-01d
**Scope:** Discussion
