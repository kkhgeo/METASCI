### `remined-intro-1` — convergence 1
A research question must be debatable. A question whose answer is simply right or wrong is a factual query, not a research question — the gap you name has to be one that argument can occupy.

> "questions that will lead to answers that are either right or wrong and therefore not debatable are (usually) not suitable as research questions."

**Sources (1):** b3-lund

### `remined-intro-2` — convergence 1
Test the research question against three attributes — specificity, originality, and relevance to a broad scientific community — and where the design allows, phrase it as a possible association between X and Y in population Z.

> "What is a good research question? The key attributes are: (i) specificity; (ii) originality or novelty; and (iii) general relevance to a broad scientific community."

**Sources (1):** perneger-2004

### `remined-intro-3` — convergence 3
Carry a thesis statement: one or two sentences that state your position and predict how the paper will develop it, placed at the end of the opening and marked with a signal phrase.

> "A thesis statement is a sentence or sentences that present your position on the topic and predict how the topic will be developed in the rest of the essay."

### `remined-intro-4` — convergence 1
The introduction must tell the reader what *this paper* is about, not how important the general field is. Readers will not wait three pages to find out what you are talking about.

> "Be sure that the introduction lets the reader know what this paper is about, not just how important your general area of research is. Readers won't stick with you for three pages to find out what you are talking about."

### `remined-intro-7` — convergence 1
Anti-pattern — the development chronicle. An introduction that recounts what your lab built, which earlier versions you struggled with, and what the system is called is a history of your group, not of the problem. Open on the problem and the contribution.

> "Example bad introduction: 'Here at the institute for computer research, me and my colleagues have created the SUPERGP system and have applied it to several toy problems. We had previously fumbled with earlier versions of SUPERGPSYSTEM for a while.'"

### `remined-intro-8` — convergence 1
Three reasons are enough to motivate the work, and each should be stated very briefly. Piling on motivation material buys nothing.

> "Three reasons are enough -- and they should be described very briefly."

### `remined-intro-9` — convergence 1

### `remined-intro-11` — convergence 1
Related Work is a survey of a much narrower slice than the field, kept short relative to the rest of the paper, and placed in exactly one position — after the introduction, or immediately before the summary. Not both.

> "Most research papers contain a 'related work' section that can be considered a survey, but it is usually brief compared to the rest of the paper and only addresses a much narrower slice of the field."

### `remined-intro-12` — convergence 1
Cite the venue you are submitting to. For a conference, cover the programme committee's relevant work and the previous two proceedings; for a journal, the last two or three volumes.

> "In the case of a conference, make sure to cite the work of the PC co-chairs and as many other PC members as are remotely plausible, as well as from anything relevant from the previous two proceedings. In the case of a journal or magazine, cite anything relevant from last 2-3 years or so volumes."

### `remined-intro-14` — convergence 1
Do not cite every article on the topic — a careful selection of the most pertinent is enough. And do not state universal truths that read as over-simple or self-evident.

> "It is not necessary to cite every article in the literature on the topic; a careful selection of the most pertinent publications is sufficient. Similarly, it is not necessary to state universal truths that may seem over simplistic or eminently obvious."

### `remined-intro-16` — convergence 2
Write the introduction last. Once the body exists, retrace what each interpretation in it assumes, and build the background the reader actually needs to follow it.

> "To write the introductory section, one can retrace each interpretation of articles in the body and compose a corresponding background explanation of substance the reader would need to understand."

### `remined-intro-18` — convergence 1

### `remined-intro-19` — convergence 1
The review has to establish that the work is new, significant, and not a repetition of something already done under similar conditions. An incomplete review that fails this is a named cause of rejection — your position and publication record will not stand in for it.

> "The literature review was incomplete and failed to establish that the proposed research was new and significant and not repetitive, i.e., the work being proposed had already been carried out in similar ecological and socio-economic conditions."

### `remined-intro-20` — convergence 1
Know where a citation sits in the literature hierarchy. Primary literature carries competing and often contradictory claims and is not yet consensual; what survives scrutiny becomes secondary literature, which is more consensual and more usable as settled background.

> "Primary literature is not entirely consensual because it contains many competing, and often contradictory, ideas and theories."

### `remined-intro-22` — convergence 1
Write objectives with verbs that name an expected outcome — *to increase*, *to decrease*, *to reduce* — rather than vague ones like *to provide*, *to establish*, *to create*. And set them off visually with bullets, numbers, or indentation so they are not buried in prose.

> "The use of such terms as \"to increase\", \"to decrease\", \"to reduce\" are more convincing and indicative of anticipated outcomes than such terms as \"to provide\", \"to establish\", or \"to create\"."

**Sources (1):** b1-iufro
**Scope:** Introduction & Literature Review

### `remined-intro-23` — convergence 1
Do not chain objectives so that one depends on a particular outcome of another — unless the design is explicitly a decision tree. Reviewers look for this specifically.

> "Avoid the trap of setting hypotheses or objectives in sequence such that one objective is dependent on a particular outcome of another objective. This can be very dangerous indeed, and reviewers usually look for this."

### `remined-intro-24` — convergence 1
Keep objectives distinct from goals, and hypotheses testable. Goals are conceptual, ultimate, abstract; objectives are tangible and time-bounded. A proposed answer that cannot be tested is useless for further investigation.

> "Do not confuse objectives with goals. The latter are conceptual, ultimate and more abstract."

### `remined-intro-26` — convergence 2
The introduction has five jobs: define the context, identify the area of study, define the topic and key terms, state the purpose, and signpost what is covered where.

> "Generally, the introduction should: define the context or background of the topic or subject; identify the area of study; define the topic and/or key terms being explored; indicate the purpose of the writing; provide signposting as to what will be covered and where"
