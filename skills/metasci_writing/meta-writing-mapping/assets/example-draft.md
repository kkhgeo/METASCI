# Separating nitrate sources from denitrification in an alluvial aquifer

## 1 Introduction

Nitrate contamination of alluvial aquifers threatens rural drinking water supplies. Shallow wells in agricultural valleys often exceed drinking-water limits. The problem is most acute where fertilizer and manure are applied on the same fields.

Previous studies have used dual nitrate isotopes to separate fertilizer and manure sources. The δ15N and δ18O of nitrate differ between these sources (Kendall, 1998). Bayesian mixing models now turn these differences into source shares (Xue et al., 2009).

However, denitrification overprints the source signal, and few studies have estimated source contributions and denitrification together. Ignoring it shifts estimates toward isotopically heavy sources.

To address this gap, we combined dual isotopes with a Bayesian mixing model to estimate source contributions and the extent of denitrification together. We applied the approach along a single valley aquifer.

## 2 Methods

The study area is a riverside alluvial aquifer, where 42 wells were sampled twice. Wells were grouped into upstream and downstream reaches. Dissolved oxygen was measured in the field.

Nitrate isotopes were analysed by the denitrifier method (Sigman et al., 2001). Replicates agreed within 0.3‰.

Source contributions were estimated with MixSIAR, and denitrification with a Rayleigh model (Stock et al., 2018). Source end-members were taken from local samples.

## 3 Results

Nitrate concentrations increased from 3.1 mg/L upstream to 18.4 mg/L downstream. The increase was steepest below the confluence.

In a group of wells, δ15N and δ18O increased together with a slope of about 1.3:1. The remaining wells showed no such trend.

The mixing model placed the manure contribution highest, at 62%, in the downstream reach. Fertilizer dominated upstream.

Several downstream wells combined low dissolved oxygen with low nitrate. These wells lay close to the river.

## 4 Discussion

The 1.3:1 slope cannot be explained by source mixing alone and points to ongoing denitrification. Similar slopes have been attributed to denitrification elsewhere (Kendall, 1998). This is the overprinting the introduction raised.

The low nitrate in low-oxygen wells is explained by the same process. Reducing conditions near the river favour it.

Recalculated as a corrected contribution, the manure share falls to 48%, close to earlier reports. Xue et al. (2009) reported comparable shares in a similar setting.

The corrected contribution is the mixing-model share after removing the denitrified fraction, and it is sensitive to the assumed enrichment factor. We report the range across plausible factors.

Rainfall and groundwater levels in the study area vary strongly with the seasons. Water tables peak in late summer.

## 5 Conclusions

Unless denitrification is separated, manure sources are overestimated, so both processes must be estimated together. The approach transfers to other valley aquifers with mixed land use.
